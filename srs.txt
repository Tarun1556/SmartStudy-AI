AI Lecture Notes Summarizer & Study Guide Builder: Hackathon Project Brief
1. Problem Context
Students collect lecture material all semester in scattered forms: recordings, slide decks, handwritten
notes, and shared drives. By exam time, they must re-watch hours of video or skim dozens of slides
to find what matters, and they often can't tell which topics the professor kept returning to. Existing
tools either transcribe a single lecture or summarize a single document. Few connect material across
a whole semester or point out which themes recur, so students end up re-reading everything instead
of studying efficiently.
2. Objective
Build a web app where a student uploads lecture recordings and slide decks over a semester, and the
AI turns them into clean structured notes per lecture. It then merges recurring themes into one study
guide that highlights the most frequently covered topics, with PDF export and a searchable archive.
3. Core Features (Must-Have)
• Upload and organize: Upload audio/video files (or paste a transcript) and slide decks
(PDF/PPTX), tagged with a course name and lecture number or date.
• Transcription and text extraction: Convert speech to text, and pull text out of slides, so every
upload becomes plain text the AI can work with.
• Structured notes per lecture: The AI produces notes with a title, headings, bullet-point key
ideas, definitions, and examples.
• Topic extraction: For each lecture, the AI outputs a list of main topics/concepts as structured
data (e.g., a JSON list), which the merging step relies on.
• Cross-lecture merged study guide: Similar topics from different lectures are grouped into
one section, so a topic like "Binary Search Trees" combines what was said in lectures 3, 5,
and 9.
• Frequency highlighting: Each topic shows how many lectures mention it (e.g., "Appears in 6
of 12 lectures"), and the most frequent ones are flagged as "likely important."
• PDF export and searchable archive: Download the study guide as a PDF, and search across
all saved notes by keyword, with results linking back to the source lecture.
4. Stretch Features (Good-to-Have)
• Auto-generated practice questions: Create flashcards or quiz questions from the top topics,
so the guide doubles as a revision tool.
• Timestamp links: Each note points to the exact moment in the recording where the topic
was discussed.
• Semantic ("meaning-based") search: Find notes by concept, not just exact words. Searching
"how databases stay consistent" would find notes about ACID properties.
• "Ask your notes" chat: A small Q&A box that answers questions using only the student's
uploaded material, with citations to lectures.

6. Suggested Approach / Step-by-Step Plan
1. Set up and split roles (Hour 0–2): Create the repo, choose the stack, get API keys, and assign
roles (e.g., frontend, backend, AI pipeline, demo/data).
2. Build the text-ingestion pipeline: Write functions that take a slide deck or a recording and
return plain text. Test with one short PDF and one short audio clip first.
3. Generate notes for one lecture: Craft a prompt that turns a transcript into structured notes
plus a JSON list of topics. Get this working well on a single lecture before anything else, as
the whole project depends on it.
4. Handle long lectures: Split long transcripts into chunks (e.g., 10 minutes each), summarize
each chunk, then combine the chunk summaries into the final lecture notes.
5. Set up the database and upload flow: Store courses, lectures, transcripts, notes, and topics.
Build the upload page and connect it to the pipeline.

6. Merge topics across lectures: Convert each topic to an embedding, group topics whose
similarity is above a threshold, and pick a clean label for each group. Count how many
distinct lectures each group appears in.
7. Build the study guide view: Display merged topics sorted by frequency, with a badge for the
most common ones and links back to the source lectures.
8. Add search and PDF export: Turn on full-text search over notes, then generate the study
guide as HTML and convert it to PDF.
9. Polish and prepare the demo (last 6–8 hours): Load a prepared set of 4–6 lectures, fix rough
UI edges, add loading indicators and error messages, and rehearse.
10. Buffer and stretch goals: Use leftover time for one stretch feature, such as practice
questions.
7. Sample Data / APIs to Use
• MIT OpenCourseWare (ocw.mit.edu): Full courses with lecture videos, slide decks, and notes
under open licenses. It is ideal for a realistic mini-semester (pick one course and use 5–6
lectures).
• CS50 lectures (Harvard): Freely available recordings and slides with a clear, structured
teaching style.
• YouTube lecture captions: The youtube-transcript-api Python library can fetch existing
captions for many public lecture videos, letting you skip transcription while developing.
• TED-LIUM corpus: A public speech dataset of TED talks with transcripts, useful for testing the
transcription and summarization steps on spoken content.
• Whisper API / open-source Whisper: For transcribing your own audio.
• Mock data option: Record two or three 3–5 minute "mini lectures" yourselves on
overlapping topics, or paste transcripts of existing lectures. Overlap is what lets you
demonstrate cross-lecture merging.
Tip: Check each source's license and only use material you're allowed to use for a demo.
8. Key Challenges to Anticipate
• Long lectures exceed what the AI can handle at once. Tip: Chunk the transcript, summarize
each chunk, then summarize the summaries. This pattern is called "map-reduce"
summarization.
• The same topic gets different names across lectures (e.g., "hash table" vs. "hashing" vs.
"dictionary structure"). Tip: Use embeddings plus a similarity threshold to group them, and
tune the threshold on real sample data. Let the AI name each merged group.
• Transcription is slow and uploads can be large. Tip: Use short clips for development, process
files in the background with a progress indicator, and pre-process your demo lectures ahead
of time.

• "Appears most often" doesn't guarantee "will be on the exam", and the AI can make things
up. Tip: Label results as "frequently covered" rather than promising exam relevance, and
always show which lectures each note came from so students can verify.
9. What a Good Demo Looks Like
Start with a folder of five or six pre-loaded lectures from one course, then upload a fresh lecture live
to show the pipeline turning it into clean, structured notes within a minute or two. Next, open the
merged study guide and show a topic that combines content from several lectures, with a frequency
badge like "Appears in 5 of 6 lectures," making the "most important topics" idea visible at a glance.
Finish by searching a keyword to jump straight to matching notes and clicking "Export PDF" to
download a polished study guide. Judges should walk away seeing the full journey: scattered
material in, one usable revision document out.
